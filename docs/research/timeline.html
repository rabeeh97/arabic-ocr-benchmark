Phase 1

Before the academic turn

We started with a benchmark and a few fine-tunes, to see how well anything reads Arabic handwriting.

Step 1

Build a benchmark and sweep models

Why
Before fine-tuning anything we needed to know how existing systems read Arabic handwriting, with numbers we could trust.
What we did
Built a harness that runs any OCR system on the same lines and scores it on a ladder: raw, light normalisation, no diacritics, and dotless skeleton. Test sets: KHATT, Muharaf, NAKBA, RASAM-1 and RASAM-2, and subsets of KITAB-Bench. Swept paid and free-tier VLMs, local models, PaddleOCR and Tesseract, with caching and cost review before any paid run.
What came out
Muharaf was the hardest handwriting set for every model (best single model 34 % CER, against 5.5 % on KHATT). A general vision-language model, Qwen3-VL-4B, was unusable on cursive: 77 to 119 % CER.

Step 2

Fine-tune a VLM on one handwriting set

Why
If the base model fails, does a modest amount of in-domain handwriting fix it, and does the fix carry to scripts it never saw?
What we did
LoRA-fine-tuned Qwen3-VL-4B for one epoch on 8,000 Muharaf lines and tested it on KHATT, NAKBA and the two Maghrebi RASAM sets.
What came out
A large, transferable gain: 47 to 67 points on every held-out set, including Maghrebi manuscripts that look nothing like Muharaf.

Step 3

Choose the base model, and unfreeze the vision encoder

Why
Is a model built for Arabic a better starting point, and is the frozen vision encoder holding the fine-tune back?
What we did
Ran the same recipe on three bases (Qwen3-VL-4B, the Arabic-specialised Baseer-Nakba, PaliGemma-2-3B), then repeated the Qwen run with the vision encoder trained too.
What came out
Specialised was not better: Baseer-Nakba already read NAKBA at 1.0 % (it was trained on it) and got worse after our recipe (3.4 %). Unfreezing the encoder helped everywhere, most on NAKBA, and it fixed letter identity more than dots (the frozen RASAM rows used only 20 lines, so those two are noisy).

Step 4

Find out which scores were not real

Why
Some numbers looked too good or too bad, and we could not tell whether the model or the harness was responsible.
What we did
Audited the pipeline: where in the line errors fall, how long outputs were against the token limits, whether test lines also sat in training, and how images were stored.
What came out
Several scores were artefacts. Models were cut off at token limits (gemini-flash dropped the tail of 44 to 46 % of lines), page-scale sets hit a 160-token cap, 86.5 % of one KITAB subset sat word for word in Muharaf train, and the public Muharaf copy is stored at only 60 px line height.

Phase 2

Turning it into research

We read the closest papers, characterised the data we had, and probed where models look.

Step 5

Read the closest papers and borrow their yardstick

Why
We wanted to characterise Arabic script the way English had been characterised, and a group at Luleå had published four connected studies on the same data.
What we did
Mapped what each paper measures to what we could measure. Recomputed their similar-substitution metric on their own published predictions, then on our models over the same lines.
What came out
Their metric reproduced exactly (4.17 %). VLMs did not confuse dots less than the CRNN: per character they made as many similar-letter errors, with a lower share only because they made two to five times more of every other kind. Only 6 % of substitutions fell in our hand-drawn shape groups and 13 % were dot pairs, so the groups need to be learned.

Step 6

Characterise the data we already had

Why
Before adding experiments, learn what the existing readings can already tell us about why lines are hard.
What we did
Re-scored every reading on the , , and on text and image features across complete model blocks.
What came out
Dots are a small tax (median 1.8 CER points, 8 % of error). 60 % of spaces wrongly inserted inside words sit at gaps between word parts. Difficulty belongs to the line (split-half reliability 0.54 to 0.81), and image features lift the explained variance from 0.06–0.51 to 0.15–0.63.

How to read the two bars

Every line has a difficulty: the median error over a group of models that all read exactly that line. The question is how much of the difference in difficulty between lines some simple facts about a line can predict.

Text features alone use only the transcription, with no picture (length, letters with dots, word parts and so on). They account for 6 to 51 % of the differences in difficulty, depending on the dataset. 0 % would mean the text tells us nothing about which lines are hard, and 100 % would mean it tells us everything.

Text + image features add facts about the picture (ink density, stroke width, line height and so on). The share rises to 15 to 63 %, which is 7 to 27 points more, so how a line looks explains a real part of why it is hard.

This does not mean text causes 6 to 51 % of the errors. It means how much of the spread between lines these facts can predict. The rest (37 to 85 % in the combined fit) is difficulty that neither kind of feature captures. The ranges run from the lowest to the highest of ten line sets, not over models. Tap a number in the chart for every set.

This only makes sense because difficulty belongs to the line: different models tend to find the same lines hard (split-half reliability 0.54 to 0.81).

What we learned, and how we used it
  • Dots are a small share of the error (about 8 %), and nine tenths remains without them. So we scored every later result on the ladder, kept dots as a separate and smaller question, and turned to letter skeletons: which shapes and parts of letters fail (see the steps on letter shapes and teeth).
  • Extra spaces sit at word-part gaps. So we measured word gaps against part gaps directly, and found they overlap: spacing is visually ambiguous. Word-part-aware decoding stays an open idea.
  • Difficulty belongs to the line. So we studied hard lines instead of averages: a line-by-system matrix, a queue of lines no system reads that goes to label review, and later item-response difficulty and challenge subsets.
  • Image features add real explanatory power (7 to 27 points), but single coefficients are unstable. So we looked for causes in the picture and tested each one directly: we measured how the datasets look, transformed lines to test each property, and found the loose crops, instead of trusting any one regression coefficient.
  • Text alone predicts little for most sets, but we did not assume text distance was unimportant. We tested it directly, with a language-model decoder and a lexicon.

Step 8

Probe where a vision-language model looks

Why
An idea on the table was to protect or freeze the weights that matter for a given letter. Before guessing which weights, check where the model looks for that letter.
What we did
On Qwen3-VL-4B with our fine-tuned adapter, computed attention, relative attention and gradient × (input − paper) maps for each generated token, hid the patches a map pointed at, and ran a pointing game against the aligned letter spans. Tried NVIDIA's LocateAnything-3B to find words and letters zero-shot.
What came out
Three maps pass: hiding what they point to removes 4.3 to 5.1 nats of the token's log-probability, against 0.8 for random patches, and one puts 9× more attribution on ink than chance. Wrong tokens differ from correct ones in confidence, not in how sharp their maps are, so errors are misreadings of the right place. LocateAnything could not find an Arabic word, letter or dot.

Phase 3

Measuring the writing itself

We measured slant, strokes and letter shapes, then tried fixes and learned which ones survive scrutiny.

Step 9

Measure how the writing differs between datasets

Why
Datasets differ visibly. Which physical properties differ enough to explain why a model trained on one fails on another?
What we did
Built detectors for (stroke orientation), , , , , and , and checked each against the transcriptions and with overlays on real lines.
What came out
Slant is the clearest difference: KHATT is upright while Muharaf leans right, and 41 % of KHATT lines sit where under 5 % of training lines do. Pen contrast separates Maghrebi (0.5–0.6) from the rest (about 1.0). Tone, noise and ruled lines differ a lot, but contour histograms that identify a dataset 70–73 % of the time do not track the transfer gap. Step 10

Make one dataset look like another, and see if the errors follow

Why
Measuring differences does not show they matter. A causal test is to transform lines until their measurements match another dataset, then re-read them.
What we did
Fitted a few knobs (shear, stroke width, sharpening, grey levels, noise) on 100 lines so twelve measures matched the target dataset, with single-step ablations, then re-scored the CRNN on the transformed lines.
What came out
The calibration works (mean gap to the target 0.77 SD before, 0.23 after). The effects on errors are modest: shearing Muharaf upright raises CER from 6.2 to 8.2 %, shearing KHATT toward Muharaf's slant lowers it from 26.3 to 25.3 %, and thinning NAKBA's strokes to Muharaf's width lowers it from 13.6 to 12.5 %.

Step 11

Catalogue augmentations and preview them on real lines

Why
The measurements suggested training augmentations, but an augmentation that changes what the line says silently poisons training.
What we did
Listed 18 candidates, from the literature and our own, and rendered each on real KHATT, Muharaf and RASAM-1 lines with the label underneath, judging by eye whether the label still fits. Some candidates have a guard that rejects a variant (stroke thickening that would merge dots).
What came out
A reviewed catalogue with a verdict per idea. Only two have been trained so far (slant and stroke width). The rest wait for the characterisation to say which are worth a GPU night.

Step 12

Train with slant and stroke augmentation

Why
If slant and stroke width differ across datasets, a model trained on varied slant and stroke should travel better.
What we did
Retrained the CRNN with random shear (−12° to +20°) and fractional stroke thickening and thinning applied on the GPU, and tested it on KHATT, NAKBA and NAKBA-blind.
What came out
It became slant-robust (an upright Muharaf line costs +0.6 points instead of +2.0), but it hurt on unseen sets: NAKBA test +0.84, blind +0.79 and KHATT +0.59 points. Kept as a robustness default, not a gain.

Step 13

Notice that loose crops hurt, and crop to the ink

Why
A table of the worst KHATT lines showed writing filling a thin strip of a tall image: 10 % of lines had ink in under 35 % of the height, at 85 % CER.
What we did
Cropped each line to the band holding its ink before resizing and re-read the lines. Later corrected our first number, which had excluded the 17 worst crops by a length filter.
What came out
KHATT 31.0 → 23.3 % on all 200 lines, for every CRNN, with no retraining. The largest gain we found. VLMs are unaffected, though fine-tuned ones lose a little on loose crops.

Step 14

Ask whether unusual letter shapes explain the errors

Why
After the crop, what remains? The letters themselves might look unlike anything in training.
What we did
Aligned each line to its letters with CTC forced alignment, gave each letter a feature vector, and measured its distance to the nearest training letter of the same character and position. Built a bank of 75,182 training letters and clustered it into allographs.
What came out
Letter-shape novelty is the strongest error predictor we found: letter error climbs from 1–5 % in the most typical fifth to 30–53 % in the most novel band, in every dataset, confirmed with a second model's features.

Why
If novel shapes cause errors, adding training lines that contain them should help.
What we did
Chose lines by shape novelty using half of the KHATT data (nothing from the other half), repeated them three times in training, and compared with the same number of random repeats, on the held-out half.

Phase 4

Making the numbers trustworthy

We looked line by line, caught our own mistakes, and wrote down a plan with rules for every result.

Step 19

Go line by line, and correct our own mistakes

Why
Mean scores hide how errors are distributed. We needed per-line and per-letter evidence for every claim.
What we did
Studied the distribution of lines, every comparison against the seed-noise yardstick, a line × system matrix, 18 pattern detectors, where spaces fall, which letters move, joint predictors of difficulty, and whether confusions follow sound or look. Kept a corrections log.
What came out
Twenty lines are hard for every system and feed the label review. Spaces inserted inside words fall at gaps between word parts (87 of 89 pairs). Confusions follow look, not sound. The log recorded that our first crop number hid the worst crops, that PaddleOCR's output was reversed by a flag, and that one model's score (AIN) was set aside until a memorisation check is done.

Step 20

Test whether the shape library is feasible

Why
A library of real letter shapes could feed synthesis and a shape-token recogniser, but only if letters can be cut out reliably.
What we did
Checked the CTC letter spans against the ink and counted how often a line's ink components match its label's word parts.
What came out
Spans are narrow anchors, not cut lines (median 12 px at 64 px height), and handwritten word parts often touch: ink components match the label's parts exactly on only 6–9 % of lines. A library needs alignment and ink together.

Why
By now there were far more threads than we could hold. Each needed a place, a grade, and a rule for what result would change what.

Step 27

Characterise skeletons, spacing, confusions and word parts

Why
With the numbers trustworthy, return to the original question: what makes Arabic script hard for a recogniser?
What we did
Measured gap widths, teeth, loops and tails, dot-writing styles, vertical stacking, a similarity-choice model of confusions, an inventory of word parts, tokenizer views and root sharing.
What came out
Word and part gaps overlap, so spacing is visually ambiguous. Teeth beat the letter baseline in all six model families. Confusability is shared across families and follows shape. An 8,000-type inventory of word parts covers 95.9 to 98.4 % of tokens, and real-word substitutions share roots more than expected.

What we use it for

The dot tax is the drop between the no-diacritics rung and the rasm rung: error that only the dots explain. Here it is 2.2 points (27.7 → 25.5 %), and about nine tenths of the error is still there once the dots are gone.

Why it matters

Counting is not enough. Because every edit has a place and a kind, we can ask where errors fall (the end of the line, the gaps inside words, dots) and what kind they are. That is how we found that spaces cost more than dots. It also shows why CER can pass 100 %: insertions have no ceiling.

Regressed line difficulty

Each line has a difficulty: how badly the models read it, taken as the median error over a block of models that all read exactly the same lines. A regression predicts that number from properties of the line, by fitting a weight for each property. If the properties predict difficulty well, they explain why a line is hard.

Why there are no model-by-dataset rows

Difficulty is one consensus value per line, taken over a group of models that all read it, so each dataset has one value per feature set, not one per model. Per-model values would answer a different question (what makes this model fail).

How much to trust each row

The line counts matter: most sets have 60 to 180 lines, so single values are noisy, while NAKBA blind (2,669) and Muharaf (472) are the most stable. ¹ KITAB-Muharaf overlaps Muharaf's training text. ² Hindawi lines are page-scale. ³ NAKBA lines has only 62 lines, so its 51 % is the least certain value. They come from our first image-layer analysis (scripts/image_layer.py), over complete blocks of models that all read the same lines.

How each detector works

Each detector below was run on one real line from each of three datasets, using the same code as the measurements in the ledger. Every picture is the detector's own output drawn on the line, with the numbers it produced, and each section says where the same property was reused in augmentation or training.

Slant

How it is computed. The writing is thinned to a one-pixel skeleton. At every skeleton pixel the stroke direction is estimated (a structure tensor on the blurred skeleton), near-vertical strokes are kept, and the median angle is the line's slant (positive leans right). A second estimator shears the whole line by trial angles from −30° to +30° and keeps the shear that makes the vertical projection most peaked: upright writing stacks its strokes into sharp columns.

Orange ticks: the stroke direction on every near-vertical skeleton pixel of the line.

The second estimator: sharpness of the vertical projection at each trial shear.

Also used in training and tests

The transfer test moved a line to another dataset's slant with one shear knob (here −11.6° to look like KHATT); training with random shear from −12° to +20°; and slant test-time augmentation.

Stroke width and pen contrast

How it is computed. The ink mask is thinned to a skeleton. At each skeleton pixel the distance to the nearest paper pixel is half the local stroke width, and the 10–90 % trimmed mean of those values (times two) is the line's stroke width. Splitting the skeleton by direction gives the width of near-vertical strokes and of near-horizontal strokes. Pen contrast is vertical ÷ horizontal: about 1 for an evenly held pen, much lower when the pen leaves thick horizontals and thin verticals, as in Maghrebi hands.

Blue: near-vertical strokes. Orange: near-horizontal strokes. Grey: everything else.

Also used in augmentation and the transfer test

Stroke width was an augmentation (thicker or thinner ink, with a guard that rejects variants that merge dots) and a knob in the transfer test (NAKBA strokes thinned to Muharaf's width).

Dots

How it is computed. After the line is rescaled so the stroke is a fixed width, connected ink components that are small (no larger than 3.2 stroke widths across) are called dots. Their number is compared with the number of dotted letters in the transcription, which is how the detector was checked (correlation 0.31 to 0.78 per set).

Red: components called dots. Blue: the rest of the writing, as a skeleton.

On the manuscript line the detector finds far more components than there are dotted letters (stains and fragments), which is why dots are treated as a rough statistic and dot pairs as one component.

Also used in augmentation

Preview of removing or moving small components (dots), used to judge whether an augmentation keeps the label valid.

Baseline

How it is computed. In sliding windows along the line, take the row with the most body ink (dots removed), then fit a straight line through those rows with robust, trimmed least squares. Two other estimators (a rotation search, and the lowest horizontal strokes on the skeleton) give a cross-check.

Green: the fitted baseline.

The baseline is the reference line for heights (ascenders and descenders measured in stroke widths) and for the loci zones. Baseline slope itself did not change recognition, and it is not used in training.

Paper tone

How it is computed. The ink mask splits the original greys into ink and paper, and the median of each is reported. The paper median says how light the page is, the ink median how dark the pen, and the gap between them is the contrast.

Grey levels (0 black, 255 white), square-rooted so the ink is visible next to the huge paper peak.

Also used in augmentation and the transfer test

Grey levels were knobs in the transfer test (ink grey, paper grey, noise) and the photometric augmentation (gamma, contrast, noise). Tone differs a lot between datasets but did not change recognition.

Ruled lines

How it is computed. After rescaling, connected ink components that are much wider than a word (over 45 stroke widths) and no taller than three stroke widths are called ruled lines or table borders and removed. The share of ink removed is recorded.

This is a cleaning step inside the other measurements. Removing ruled lines did not change recognition, and they are not used in training.

Contour loci

How it is computed. For every background pixel, look in the four directions (north, south, east, west) and count how many separate runs of ink you meet, capped at two. The four counts give one of 81 codes, and the histogram of codes over the line, split into zones above, near and below the baseline, describes its shape. Such histograms identify a line's dataset 70 to 73 % of the time, but they do not track the transfer gap.

The 81 codes, in three rows: above, near and below the baseline. Darker means more background pixels with that code (this KHATT line).

Used to describe and compare datasets. It is not part of any augmentation or training.
